89 Cents

The creator earned eighty nine cents. Honey took thirty five dollars.

A plain record of what was found out about Honey, kept here so it stays in one place and stays readable.

None of this investigation is ours. It was done by MegaLag, a New Zealand investigative journalist, over several years, and verified in part by Ben Edelman, a researcher who has spent two decades documenting affiliate fraud. His third video is called The Honey Files. This page is only a record of what he and Edelman found, written in one place so it stays readable.

Clover exists because of this story. Everything below is why.

Part one

Exposing the Honey Influencer Scam

21 December 2024 · 23 minutes · past 9 million views in days

1The hidden tab

At checkout, Honey opened a discrete tab in the corner of the browser and injected a simulated referral click, so the sale was credited to Honey. This is called cookie stuffing. It replaced the affiliate cookie of whoever had actually sent you to the shop.

2It did this even with no coupon

The link was overwritten whether or not Honey found a working code. So a tool that failed to save you anything still took the commission from the person whose recommendation sent you there.

3Thirty five dollars against eighty nine cents

On one purchase, going through the creator's own affiliate link earned about thirty five dollars. The same purchase made with Honey's rewards active earned eighty nine cents in cashback. The rest went to PayPal.

4Better codes were hidden

Partner shops could tell Honey which codes to display. If a thirty percent code and a five percent code both existed, the five percent one is what you saw. The pitch was always the best coupon. Codes were also found on other coupon sites at times when Honey reported none existed.

5It hit the people advertising it

Linus Tech Tips had promoted Honey across more than a hundred and sixty videos totalling close to two hundred million views. MrBeast and MKBHD had promoted it too. Their own audiences, buying through their own links, were losing them commission. Markiplier had turned down a Honey sponsorship back in 2020, saying only that he did not trust them.

Exposing Honey's Evil Business Model

6Shops that never agreed

The extension described shops as participating. Most of them had no agreement with Honey at all and had never been asked.

7Codes taken from users

When you typed a code Honey did not recognise, it asked permission to share it with everyone else. The allegation is that it took them regardless. Around sixty two percent of the coupon library came from users and manual entry, not from the clever scanning the advertising described.

8Codes that were never meant to be public

Staff discounts, refund codes, one-off promotions, and in some cases codes giving a hundred percent off. One business said it cost them thousands before they noticed. Shops that asked to be removed say they were told the codes would come out if they became paying affiliates.

9The six minute window

Affiliate rules require tools like Honey to stand down for a period after someone else's referral. Archived copies of Honey's own code show that window was originally set to six minutes. After the first video went viral it was quietly raised to an hour. Six minutes means that if you clicked a creator's link, browsed for seven minutes and then bought, the credit was taken.

10Advertising aimed at children

The video documented Honey marketing placed in front of minors.

Part three

The Honey Files Expose Major Fraud

30 December 2025 · with Ben Edelman

This is the part that changes the story from a company being greedy to a company knowing exactly what it was doing.

11Selective stand-down

Honey followed the stand-down rules properly when it believed it was being watched, and ignored them when it believed it was not. Edelman extracted the source code, captured the configuration files with a packet sniffer, and cross-checked both against how the extension actually behaved and against archived versions going back to 2017.

12How it decided you were a tester

Four things were checked. How old your account was. How many cashback points you had. A list held on Honey's servers. And whether your browser held cookies from affiliate networks such as CJ or Awin, which is to say whether you had ever logged into an affiliate dashboard. If you looked like an insider, Honey behaved perfectly.

Edelman's conclusion: it is inconceivable why a shopping plugin should care whether a user had previously logged into an affiliate dashboard. There is only one reason to check, and that is to hide from testers. Which means the company knew the behaviour was not allowed.

13The thresholds moved after the first video

The points threshold was raised to sixty five thousand, putting it out of reach of most testers. But a separate rule for Rakuten requiring only five thousand points was left behind, and that oversight let researchers demonstrate the whole system still working.

14A switch on their end

The system could be turned off globally from Honey's servers at any moment, without users or affiliate networks seeing anything change. A week after Edelman published, the selective stand-down quietly disappeared from the configuration files. He called that a kind of admission.

Separately

What it was collecting

15The GDPR test

Honey's policy said it only collected data on shopping sites it supported, that nothing identified you personally, and that nothing was ever sold. In 2020 a German privacy group had two members exercise their right to request their own data. What came back did not match the policy: browsing history at scale, with timestamps, location and page addresses, tied to a user id, including for someone who had never created an account. They filed complaints with the data protection authorities.

16Amazon's warning

Amazon put a notice on its own site telling shoppers the extension tracked their private shopping behaviour and could read or change data on any website they visited, and advising them to uninstall it immediately. Worth knowing that PayPal had just bought Honey for four billion dollars and Amazon is a competitor, so the timing suited them. The wording was still specific rather than vague.

2026

Thrown out, then let back in

The networks that Honey had been taking commissions through finally acted, and then the story turned in a way nobody expected.

17The evidence in the court filing

A second complaint was filed on 5 January 2026, a hundred and one pages with actual merchant contracts attached. Paragraph 253 says internal emails between Rakuten and PayPal show Honey repeatedly violated Rakuten's stand down policies from as early as July 2020, and kept doing it after the lawsuits were filed, through at least May 2025. Two months earlier a judge had refused PayPal's attempt to move the case out of open court into private arbitration.

18Three networks cut them off

Rakuten Advertising terminated Honey on 12 January 2026, which cut it off from around two thousand shops including Walmart, Sephora, Lego, Dyson and Uniqlo. Impact.com suspended them four days later, citing stand down violations and hiding from testers. Awin ran its own investigation and confirmed the breaches on 21 January, froze payments, and blocked access to new advertisers.

19The defeat device vanished the same day

According to MegaLag, the configuration file holding the selective stand down logic was switched off at almost exactly the moment Rakuten's ban landed. His comment was that the timing would not look good in court.

20And then they were allowed back

On 6 May 2026 Rakuten published an open source tool that lets any extension check whether somebody else already earned the credit for a visit, and stand down properly. Honey installed it and was reinstated to the network the same day. No fine, no penalty, back to normal.

Ben Edelman, whose research proved the hiding in the first place, said publicly that he would have expected Honey to pay a substantial penalty for the trouble caused, the cost of the investigations, and the loss of confidence in the whole industry. He asked whether a company should simply get to install the fix and carry on as if nothing had happened.

21Why people inside the industry said it was fine

The tester detection had a side effect worth sitting with. Edelman put it plainly: when Honey suspected a user might be a tester, a network quality employee, a merchant's affiliate manager, an affiliate or just an enthusiast, the software honoured the stand down rules in full.

So anyone in a position to check was shown the version that behaved. Affiliate managers who tested it saw a well behaved extension. People who worked in the industry, and reportedly people who worked at Honey, could install it, watch it at a checkout, and honestly conclude that MegaLag had got it wrong. They were not lying. They were looking at different software.

That is the part that makes this different from ordinary corner cutting. A company can break a rule by accident. Building a system that shows the compliant version specifically to the people who might notice is not an accident, and Edelman's argument is that it proves Honey knew the behaviour was against the rules. You do not hide something you think is allowed.

The new rules are worth reading, because they describe Clover by accident. All stand down logic has to run inside the extension, not on a server, so it cannot be changed invisibly. And software must not take any action designed to hide its behaviour from testers.

Since then

What it cost

20m → 14mChrome users by July 2025
~2,000shops lost in the Rakuten ban
20+class actions against PayPal

The lawsuits allege wiretapping, computer hacking, unfair competition, consumer fraud, tortious interference and unjust enrichment. Google rewrote its rules for Chrome extensions specifically to block what Honey had been doing. Edelman has since proposed new affiliate industry rules designed to make testing easier, and found the same stand-down violations in Microsoft's own Edge shopping tool, so this was never only one company.

Honey's co-founder Ryan Hudson left PayPal in 2022 and started Pie, an ad blocker that pays you for ads you choose to see. Pie also ships a shopping extension that applies coupons and runs cashback. During setup, Pie offers to switch off your other ad blockers, and clicking that button removes uBlock Origin, AdGuard and Adblock Plus.

Why this page exists

The point of keeping the record

None of this needed a coupon tool to be bad at coupons. Honey found real codes and applied them and people did save money. Everything above happened somewhere the user could not see, to someone who was not them.

That is the whole reason Clover shows every code in the open, counts who a code actually worked for instead of wearing a badge, and never touches a link somebody else earned. If a tool ever needs you not to look at it closely, that is the tool worth looking at closely.
